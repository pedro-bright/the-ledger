## Summary

On January 21, 2024, two days before the New Hampshire presidential primary, thousands of voters received a robocall in an AI-generated voice that sounded like President Joe Biden, telling them to "save your vote for the November election."
New Hampshire's attorney general called it an unlawful attempt to suppress voters, and within three weeks investigators had traced the calls to a Texas company, while the Federal Communications Commission ruled that AI-generated voices in robocalls fall under federal robocall restrictions.
The consultant who commissioned the call was fined $6 million by the FCC but was acquitted of state criminal charges in June 2025.

## What Happened

On January 22, 2024, the New Hampshire Department of Justice announced that it had received complaints about a recorded message sent the day before that encouraged voters not to vote in the January 23 primary.
The message said "your vote makes a difference in November, not this Tuesday," and, although the voice sounded like President Biden's, it "appears to be artificially generated."
The calls were spoofed to appear to come from the number of a former state Democratic Party chair who was treasurer of a committee supporting the write-in campaign for Biden, who was not on the primary ballot.
The attorney general's office told voters that voting in the primary did not prevent them from voting in November.

On February 6, Attorney General John Formella announced that investigators, working with a task force of state attorneys general and the FCC's Enforcement Bureau, had traced the calls to Life Corporation, a Texas company, and an individual named Walter Monk, with Texas-based Lingo Telecom as the originating carrier.
The state issued a cease-and-desist order to Life Corporation, and the FCC sent one to Lingo Telecom.
The call-blocking service Nomorobo estimated that between 5,000 and 25,000 calls were made.
On February 8, the FCC unanimously adopted a declaratory ruling that calls using AI-generated voices are "artificial" under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act, which requires prior consent for such calls and gives state attorneys general an added basis to sue.

On May 23, 2024, Steven Kramer, a New Orleans political consultant, was indicted in New Hampshire on 13 felony counts of voter suppression and 13 misdemeanor counts of impersonating a candidate.
The FCC separately fined him $6 million in September 2024, and Lingo Telecom agreed to pay $1 million in a settlement.
Kramer admitted commissioning the message, testifying that he paid a New Orleans magician $150 to create it as a "wake-up call" about the dangers of AI.
On June 13, 2025, a jury acquitted him of the 11 voter suppression and 11 impersonation charges he was tried on, after his defense argued that the primary, which the Democratic National Committee had not sanctioned, was a meaningless straw poll and that the message did not impersonate a declared candidate.
Kramer told the Associated Press he would not pay the FCC fine.

## Why It Matters

This was one of the first documented uses of an AI voice clone to try to suppress votes in a US election, and it came in the first weeks of the 2024 primary season.
It drew a fast response: the source was identified within three weeks, and the FCC's ruling turned an existing consumer protection law into a tool against AI voice clones within 18 days of the calls.

The criminal acquittal showed the limits of existing election law.
The jury apparently accepted the defense's argument that the state's voter suppression and impersonation statutes did not fit the facts, so an admitted attempt to mislead voters with a cloned voice went unpunished under state criminal law.
The remaining penalty, the FCC fine, rested on federal rules about consent for robocalls, and Kramer said he would not pay it.
The incident also fits the reading of 2024 as a baseline rather than a reassurance: one of the most widely reported AI attempts to interfere with that year's elections used a recording made for $150, was quickly traced, and was penalized mainly through a telecom rule.
