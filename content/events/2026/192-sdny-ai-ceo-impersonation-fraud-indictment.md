## Summary

On November 5, 2026, the U.S. Attorney's Office for the Southern District of New York unsealed a seven-count superseding indictment charging Jacob Soall, Sharon Hazan and Guy Sheetrit with a fraud scheme that used AI to imitate the voices and at times the faces of company chief executives on phone and video calls.
According to the indictment, the scheme ran from about July 2024 to about February 2026, targeted thousands of companies and stole at least approximately $129 million through wire transfers that employees made on the instructions of people posing as their CEOs and as lawyers at large international law firms.
The three men were arrested in Spain and the United Kingdom between June and September 2026, and U.S. extradition requests are pending.

## What Happened

The indictment, captioned S3 26 Cr. 168, alleges that the conspirators usually began by sending an accounting employee an email through an electronic signature service such as DocuSign that appeared to come from the company's CEO.
The email described a confidential financial transaction, asked the employee to sign a nondisclosure agreement and directed them to call an attorney at a named law firm.
One example quoted in the indictment was sent on or about October 8, 2024, to an employee of a major investment bank headquartered in New York.
On the follow-up phone or video calls, according to the indictment, the conspirators "would use AI to mimic the voice and at times the image of the Impersonated CEO" to convince the employee that the transaction was real.
The attorneys they impersonated were real people at real firms, often among the 50 largest law firms in the world, and were generally chosen from those firms' European offices.

The person posing as the attorney would then direct the employee to wire money to an account the conspiracy controlled.
The press release lists six victims by type and location: a U.K. financial technology company ($11,578,474), a Florida pet food company ($5,242,747), a Missouri hospital system ($4,829,580), a Massachusetts mutual fund company ($4,639,918), a California hospital system ($2,968,497) and a Rhode Island bank ($2,684,580).
Funds went to accounts in the United States, Spain, Portugal, Greece, the United Arab Emirates, Singapore, Austria, Slovakia and China, and were then converted into cryptocurrency, prosecutors said.

Prosecutors allege that Soall, 41, of Marbella, Spain, and the United Kingdom, mainly impersonated U.K. attorneys and kept a handwritten script titled "Lawyer Call" for those conversations.
Hazan, 54, of Israel, and Sheetrit, 42, of Israel and the United Arab Emirates, are accused of laundering the proceeds.
According to the release, Hazan boasted that he had laundered more than $400 million in the past three years; he was convicted of conspiracy to commit bank fraud in the same district in 2008.
Soall was arrested in Spain on June 17, 2026, Hazan in Spain on August 5, and Sheetrit in the United Kingdom on September 7, with assistance from Spain's Policía Nacional and the U.K. National Crime Agency.

Each defendant faces conspiracy to commit wire fraud, wire fraud, two counts of aggravated identity theft, money laundering conspiracy and two counts of money laundering.
The release gives a maximum of 20 years for each of the fraud and money laundering counts and two years for each aggravated identity theft count.
U.S. Attorney Jamie McDonald said the defendants "abused groundbreaking super intelligence technology to impersonate real people and deceive corporate executives into sending them and their co-conspirators approximately $129 million," and urged businesses "to consider how to best protect themselves from the emerging threat of SI-enabled crime."
NBC News reported the charges on November 6.

## Why It Matters

The case puts a dollar figure and a documented method on AI voice and video impersonation used against corporate payment processes.
The indictment describes a repeatable sequence: a signature-service email, an AI-assisted call in the CEO's voice, and a handoff to an impersonated outside lawyer who requests the wire.
Hospital systems, a bank and a mutual fund company are among the named victim types.

The filing and the press release use different terms for the same technology.
The indictment says the defendants "used artificial intelligence ('AI')," while the release, issued under U.S. Attorney McDonald, says "super intelligence ('SI')" throughout, the same substitution the office made in its October 2026 sentencing release in the Michael Smith music streaming case.

The charges are allegations, and none of the defendants is yet in U.S. custody.
The press release does not name the AI tools the defendants allegedly used, and its opening paragraph puts the losses at "more than $100 million" while its later text and the indictment say $129 million.
Whether the case reaches trial depends on extradition proceedings in Spain and the United Kingdom.
