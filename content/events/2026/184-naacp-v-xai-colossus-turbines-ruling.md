## Summary

On October 30, 2026, Judge Debra M. Brown of the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Mississippi denied xAI's motion to dismiss a Clean Air Act citizen suit brought by the NAACP and its Mississippi State Conference over the Colossus Gas Plant, a site of gas-fired combustion turbines in Southaven, Mississippi, that supplies power to xAI's Colossus 2 data center in Memphis. The court held that the trailer-mounted turbines are not "mobile sources" exempt from permitting under Mississippi's State Implementation Plan, gave no deference to a 2025 letter in which the state regulator had treated them as exempt, and rejected xAI's argument that the Act's citizen-suit provision is unconstitutional. Eleven days earlier, the same judge had allowed the United States to intervene on behalf of the EPA Administrator but refused its request to dismiss the case.

## What Happened

The NAACP and the Mississippi State Conference of the NAACP filed the complaint on April 14, 2026, against X.AI Corp. and MZX Tech LLC, its wholly owned subsidiary. It alleges three violations: building and operating a stationary source without a permit under Mississippi's State Implementation Plan (SIP); building and operating a major stationary source without a Prevention of Significant Deterioration permit and without best available control technology; and operating a major source of hazardous air pollutants without complying with the federal turbine standard in NESHAP Subpart YYYY. The plaintiffs seek an injunction against operating the plant except in compliance with the Act and civil penalties of up to $124,426 per day per violation. They moved for a preliminary injunction on June 12.

As the complaint describes the site, and as the court accepted for purposes of the motion, MZX acquired the property at 2875 Stanton Road South in July 2025. That month, xAI's vice president of operations, Brent Mayo, told the Mississippi Department of Environmental Quality (MDEQ) by email that xAI planned to generate power there with combustion turbines. MDEQ replied on July 29 that it "understands that the referenced turbines will be 'mobile' as each will remain affixed to a portable unit (i.e., a flatbed trailer) and 'temporary'" because each would stay on site for less than twelve months, and that on those conditions the turbines were exempt from permitting. The turbines were installed between August 1 and December 18, 2025. As of April 7, 2026, the complaint counts at least 27 gas-fired turbines on site (fourteen SMT-130s, one M35 and twelve TM 2500s) with a combined capacity of at least 495 megawatts. The Southern Environmental Law Center, which represents the plaintiffs, has described "at least 35 combustion turbines and other sources of air pollution" at the site, according to TechCrunch.

On June 15, 2026, the United States, acting for the EPA Administrator, moved to intervene as a plaintiff and to dismiss the case, arguing that the Clean Air Act "affords [it] primacy over when and how the statute should be enforced." When the court had not ruled, the United States filed a notice of appeal to the Fifth Circuit on September 18 from what it called an August 24 "constructive Order" denying its motion, and asked the district court to stay the case; the Fifth Circuit denied the government's own request to stay the litigation. In an opinion filed October 19, Judge Brown held that the Administrator is entitled to intervene in a Clean Air Act citizen suit but that "nothing in the Clean Air Act allows the Administrator to unilaterally dismiss a citizen suit upon intervention." She noted that the Administrator "has not taken any action to enforce the Clean Air Act here," which distinguished the cases the government cited, most of which involved EPA consent decrees.

The October 30 opinion rejected each of the three grounds in xAI's motion to dismiss. On statutory standing, the court found that the NAACP could sue on behalf of its members, a point on which it said no Supreme Court, Fifth Circuit or in-circuit district court decision appeared to have addressed "the precise question here." On the permitting exemption, it held that turbines with a heat input above 10 MMBtu per hour that are not self-propelled or propelled while operating are subject to federal performance standards under 40 C.F.R. Part 60, Subpart KKKK, and "are therefore not 'nonroad engines' and not 'mobile sources.'" It declined to treat MDEQ's 2025 letter as controlling, noting that Mississippi courts do not defer to agency interpretations of statutes and that MDEQ's reading of "mobile source" "does not depend on facts within MDEQ's expertise." On the constitutional challenge, which argued that citizen suits seeking civil penalties exercise executive power in violation of Article II and the private nondelegation doctrine, the court held: "Because citizen suit plaintiffs sue on their own behalf, in their own name, and seek penalties that redress harm to themselves, they are not exercising the federal government's executive power."

The opinion addressed only the motion to dismiss and did not rule on the pending motion for a preliminary injunction. TechCrunch reported the decision on October 31 and quoted Abre' Conner, the NAACP's director of environmental and climate justice: "The courts have repeatedly rejected attempts by xAI and the DOJ to dismiss the NAACP's Clean Air Act case."

## Why It Matters

The case tests a method AI companies have used to bring power online for training clusters faster than grid connections allow: running large fleets of trailer-mounted gas turbines under exemptions for mobile or temporary equipment. The court's reading, that turbines above the Subpart KKKK size threshold are stationary sources whatever they are mounted on, is a ruling on Mississippi's federally approved SIP at the pleading stage. It accepts the complaint's facts as true and does not decide whether xAI violated the Act, what the turbines emit, or whether the plant must stop running. Other states' plans and other regulators' exemption letters were not before the court.

The federal government's position is the less common part of the record. The EPA, through the Justice Department, sought to end a citizen suit against a company that EPA itself had not acted against, and filed an appeal before the district court had ruled on its motion. The Fifth Circuit has not decided that appeal. Both the government's claim to control citizen enforcement and xAI's argument that civil-penalty citizen suits violate Article II could reach beyond this case if an appellate court accepts either one, because other federal environmental statutes, including the Clean Water Act, contain similar citizen-suit provisions.

The rulings came during a year of growing opposition to AI data center construction, including the House's 417-3 vote in September on the Ratepayer Protection Act. Still unknown as of the October 30 order: whether the court will grant a preliminary injunction, how the Fifth Circuit will rule, and whether a court-ordered permit requirement would limit power to Colossus 2. xAI has been part of SpaceX since a February 2026 merger.
