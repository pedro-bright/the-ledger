## Summary

On October 16, 2026, Anthropic published "How employment is changing at Anthropic," a report by its economics researchers on how AI agents had changed hiring and work inside the company. The report said the share of junior employees in Anthropic's technical departments fell from 21% to 14% since early 2025, and the junior share of technical hires fell from 27% to 9%. It also reported that by August 2026, AI agents directed by individual employees worked six times as many hours as the employees themselves.

## What Happened

The report, by Nathan Wilmers, Regina Catipon, Maxim Massenkoff and Peter McCrory, drew on internal employment records, AI usage telemetry, output metrics, employee surveys, Slack and calendar data, and hiring decision records, supplemented by 17 employee interviews. Anthropic framed itself as a possible "leading indicator" of how AI would change work organization and labor demand across the economy.

On hiring, the report said internal discussion of lower demand for junior technical staff began in January 2026, and that by April engineering and research teams had deemphasized hiring for lower-band technical positions. In an April manager survey, around a third of machine learning managers and a fifth of software engineering managers said they were hiring less because of AI. Postings for junior roles had fallen sharply since December 2025. Technical departments' share of total headcount fell from 61% to 56% over the year, as sales, finance and recruiting grew faster, although technical hiring still grew in absolute terms. The report said Anthropic had tripled in size in the past year and earned two to four times as much revenue per employee as Meta and Google had at a similar stage of growth.

On agent use, the report said combined autonomous and interactive agent work hours first exceeded human work hours in March 2026. Code commits per engineer were five times their level of six months earlier. Comparing 192 engineers who adopted coding agents with 74 who did not, it found output rose sharply after adoption, and found no difference in the rate at which agent-written and human-written code was later removed or reverted. The authors noted that adoption was not random, which could bias these estimates. Interactive agent sessions spent around 80% of their wall-clock time waiting for human input, and "meta-work" such as writing specifications and monitoring agent pipelines rose from 10% to 22% of employees' interactive sessions between fall 2025 and spring 2026. Median meeting time per employee rose 20% year over year, and 16% for technical individual contributors.

Hiring criteria also shifted. Interviewer comments on candidates' use of AI tools tripled for technical roles and quadrupled for non-technical roles between 2024 and 2026, and comments on autonomy and judgment grew while comments on domain expertise shrank. The report described two company responses: an internal early warning system that tracks role-level demand and productivity against AI capabilities, and a priority on redeploying staff internally. Employees with at least a year of tenure were 50% more likely to move between departments each month in 2026 than in 2025.

TechCrunch reported the findings on October 17, quoting the junior hiring figures and the meeting-time data.

## Why It Matters

Frontier AI developers had published surveys of how their staff use their own models, including earlier Anthropic surveys of its engineers, but this report added hiring, headcount and posting data on how the company's workforce changed. It extends the pattern Anthropic's March 2026 Economic Index described across the economy, reduced entry-level hiring in occupations most exposed to AI, to a single firm where agent use is unusually intensive and measured directly.

The figures are self-reported and were not independently audited. Anthropic sells the coding agents whose effects the report measures, and the authors acknowledged that much of Anthropic's practice cannot be replicated elsewhere. The report also did not publish absolute headcounts, so the size of the decline in junior hiring in numbers of people is not known. Whether other employers follow the same path, and whether internal redeployment holds up outside a fast-growing company with broad stock ownership, are questions the report itself left open.
