## Summary

On October 29, 2026, Cohere announced the initial close of its Series E, a round "targeting up to CAD $7 Billion" led by Schwarz Group, with participation from Bell Ventures, Bullhound Capital, Inovia Capital, NVIDIA, Prosperity7 Ventures and the Government of Canada.
The same day, Prime Minister Mark Carney announced that Canada would make "an equity investment of up to $1.6 billion" (Canadian dollars; Semafor put it at up to US$1.1 billion), paid in stages and tied to commitments on Canadian jobs, computing infrastructure and the company's Canadian headquarters.
Bloomberg reported a valuation of $20.5 billion.
Cohere called the round "the largest funding round ever raised by a private Canadian company."

## What Happened

Cohere disclosed the round in a post signed by its three co-founders, Aidan Gomez, Nick Frosst and Ivan Zhang, titled "Who Gets to Build the Frontier for AI."
The post named the investors but gave no figure for the amount committed at the initial close, no valuation and no breakdown by investor.
Schwarz Group, the German parent of Lidl and Kaufland, had committed €500 million (about $600 million) in structured financing in April, when Cohere agreed to acquire Aleph Alpha.
Bloomberg had reported on September 11 that Cohere was in talks to raise up to $3 billion at a valuation that could reach $20 billion.
Its October 29 headline put the closing valuation at $20.5 billion.

The Prime Minister's Office described the government's stake as "a commercial investment, announced alongside private investors, including NVIDIA, Bell, and Schwarz Group."
In return, the release said, Cohere will create "at least 1,000 new high-paying Canadian technical jobs by 2030," commit "at least $1.7 billion to additional computing infrastructure in Canada by 2030," "maintain its Canadian incorporation and Canadian headquarters," invest in Canadian research partnerships, and "keep working with the Canadian AI Safety Institute."
The investment "will be made in stages, with regular reporting on progress against the company's commitments, including jobs and investment in Canada."
Carney said that "true sovereignty depends on our ability to build, adopt, and govern AI on our own terms."
Evan Solomon, Minister of Artificial Intelligence and Digital Innovation, said the investment "sends a clear message: you should not have to leave Canada to build a company that succeeds around the world."

Most of Cohere's post was an argument for the round's purpose: "a frontier company outside the U.S. and China."
The founders called the view that frontier AI belongs to two countries and a few companies "frontier fatalism" and "a plan for managed decline."
They cited OpenRouter data showing that "more than 70 percent of the open-model tokens served through OpenRouter came from Chinese models earlier this year," up from 3 percent fourteen months earlier.
They named cybersecurity as the main domain in which Cohere intends to compete, through AISLE, a company "which will soon join us as Cohere Cyber," and said AISLE's system had identified 18 of the 24 vulnerabilities fixed in curl's October release, after curl's founder Daniel Stenberg reported in August that Anthropic's Mythos, OpenAI's Codex Security and ZeroPath had found nothing further in the codebase.
The post compared the effort to the founding of Airbus and put Canada's public spending on AI at "more than C$5 billion" since 2017.

Semafor reported the news as "Canada is taking an equity stake of up to $1.1 billion in a Toronto-based AI startup, as Washington's allies intensify efforts to reduce their technological reliance on the US," and linked it to a UK pledge the same week of $80 million for a sovereign AI fund.

## Why It Matters

The round follows Mistral's €3 billion Series D at more than €21 billion in September, and both companies raised on the same argument: that governments and regulated customers want models built outside the United States and China that they can control under their own laws.
The two valuations are close, at about $24 billion for Mistral and $20.5 billion for Cohere by Bloomberg's figure, and both sit far below those of the leading US laboratories.
Cohere's round differs in that a national government is now a direct shareholder.
The conditions attached to Canada's stake, which bind the company to Canadian incorporation and a Canadian headquarters and set hiring and compute targets for 2030, mean that a future sale, merger or move of the company would involve a government shareholder with contractual commitments behind it.

Several facts were not public at the time of the announcement.
Neither Cohere nor the government said how much was committed at the initial close, how Canada's stake is staged, or what happens if the job and compute targets are missed.
Cohere stated its target in Canadian dollars and outlets converted it to different US figures, and only Bloomberg reported a valuation.
Whether a company of Cohere's size can compete at the general-purpose frontier, which its own post concedes "requires capital, compute and research talent at a scale few organizations can assemble," is not settled by the round; the founders' emphasis on domain frontiers such as cybersecurity is in part an answer to that question.
