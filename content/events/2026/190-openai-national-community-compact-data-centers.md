## Summary

On November 5, 2026, OpenAI published two documents about the data centers it builds, which it calls compute campuses.
The eight-page National Community Compact sets out what OpenAI commits to at every future campus it owns and operates, including that it will not use non-disclosure agreements with public officials, will pay the full cost of the electricity it uses and the infrastructure built to serve it, and will publish site-level water, electricity, noise and economic data.
The 18-page Compute Campus Policy Blueprint asks federal, state and local policymakers to make similar terms binding on all developers.
The release came two days after the November 3 midterm elections, in which, according to Semafor, voters rejected data center construction in nearly every ballot measure on the subject.

## What Happened

OpenAI announced the documents in a Global Affairs post titled "Setting a new standard for responsible AI infrastructure."
The post presents them as the next step after the Stargate Community commitments OpenAI made in January 2026 to pay its own way on energy, and after the site agreements that followed in Effingham County, Georgia (Project Camellia) and Pike County, Ohio.
None of the three documents mentions the election.

The compact applies to "every campus owned and operated by OpenAI" and to future sites.
Each such campus is to get its own local compact shaped by resident input, a local point of contact, and an annual public report on progress.
On disclosure, OpenAI states: "OpenAI will not require public officials to sign NDAs as a condition for discussion or negotiation," with narrow protections kept for security information, trade secrets and competitively sensitive information.
It commits to publishing construction plans covering traffic, noise and lighting, to commissioning independent noise studies before operations begin and every year afterward, and to publishing water use by location, on-site electricity use including electricity generated on site, and site-specific economic impacts such as jobs, tax payments and community investments.

On energy, the compact says OpenAI "signed the national Ratepayer Protection Pledge" and will pay the incremental cost of the infrastructure required to serve its sites and the full cost of the electricity it uses.
Before major spending begins, it will provide financial assurances so that other utility customers are not left with costs if a project is delayed, reduced or canceled.
It also describes a "Grid Protector" role in which campuses reduce their demand during grid stress, citing OpenAI's agreement with Georgia Power for up to 1,000 megawatts of flexible demand response at the Effingham campus.
On water, it commits to a water plan for each campus and to prioritizing closed-loop or low-water cooling.
The compact lists existing community commitments of $80 million in Effingham County and $40 million in Pike County, including $4.8 million for free school meals for all 14,000 students in Effingham County and a joint $6 million childcare commitment with SB Energy in Pike County.

The blueprint states that "voluntary measures alone don't meet the scale of this moment" and asks for rules that apply to every developer.
Its proposals include prohibiting data center developers from requiring public officials to sign NDAs; directing state public utility commissions to create large-load tariffs that "require large electricity consumers to pay the incremental costs they create," which it says "should codify the national Ratepayer Protection Pledge"; requiring independent noise studies and reporting of facility electricity use and power usage effectiveness; and asking states and local governments to disclose discretionary tax incentive agreements, require measurable performance commitments and include "clawback provisions if material commitments are not met."
At the federal level it asks Congress to give the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission authority to create a faster grid connection process for ready-to-build projects, to pass permitting reform for generation and transmission, and to restore and expand technology-neutral clean electricity tax incentives.

Semafor reported the announcement as part of an effort "to render the AI buildout more palatable to the public," including the renaming of data centers as "compute campuses," and wrote that US voters "opted to check data center construction nearly everywhere it appeared on the ballot this week."
The headlines of Bloomberg ("OpenAI to Change How It Handles Data Center Efforts After Outcry") and The Washington Post ("Open AI makes pledges on data center operation after midterm backlash") also tied the documents to public opposition to data center projects.

## Why It Matters

The compact is a set of voluntary commitments from one of the largest builders of AI data centers in the United States, and the blueprint turns those commitments into proposed rules for every developer on how such facilities are priced, disclosed and connected to the grid.
The ratepayer proposal is close in substance to H.R. 9340, the Ratepayer Protection Act, which the House passed 417-3 in September 2026 and which would require state regulators to consider large-load tariffs of this kind.
The blueprint gives the reason for the NDA proposal in plain terms: "Public officials shouldn't be required to sign nondisclosure agreements that keep them from sharing essential information with their constituents."
Published two days after the election, the documents record an AI developer asking for binding rules on its own infrastructure, in the same week that voters turned down data center projects at the ballot box.

The commitments have limits that the documents themselves define.
They apply only to future campuses that OpenAI owns and operates, so campuses that partners own or operate for OpenAI fall outside them as written.
The compact names no enforcement mechanism, dispute process or independent verification of the published water and electricity figures beyond the noise studies.
Whether any of the blueprint's proposals will be adopted by state utility commissions, legislatures or Congress, and whether other developers will match the NDA commitment, was not known at the time of writing.
