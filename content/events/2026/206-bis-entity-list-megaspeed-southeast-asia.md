## Summary

On November 23, 2026, the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) added seven companies in Malaysia, Singapore and Thailand to the Entity List, among them three Megaspeed entities, including Singapore-based Megaspeed International, the GPU cloud operator at the center of the investigations surrounding Nvidia. The rule cites "the significant risk of these entities being or becoming involved in the diversion of advanced computing commodities to unauthorized end uses or end users" and requires a license, reviewed under a presumption of denial, for every item subject to the Export Administration Regulations. Semafor first reported the listing that afternoon, the same day Nvidia's board reinstated Jensen Huang as chief executive.

## What Happened

The rule, FR Doc. 2026-23999, was filed for public inspection at 4:15 p.m. Eastern time on November 23, took effect that day, and was published in the Federal Register on November 25 at 91 FR 76148. It was signed by Julia Khersonsky, Deputy Assistant Secretary for Strategic Trade. The End-User Review Committee, which the rule describes as made up of representatives of the Departments of Commerce (chair), State, Defense, Energy and, where appropriate, the Treasury, decided the additions by majority vote.

The seven entities are MSFusion Sdn. Bhd., PT Megaspeed International and Speedmatrix Sdn Bhd. in Malaysia; Apex Enterprise Solutions (Singapore) Private Limited, Megaspeed International Pte. Ltd. and Swiftdata Pte. Ltd. in Singapore; and Megaspeed (Thailand) Co., Ltd. in Thailand. The Singapore and Thailand Megaspeed entries list "7Road International" as an alias. Every entry carries a license requirement "for all items subject to the EAR" with a review policy of "presumption of denial." A savings clause lets shipments already en route on November 23 proceed if delivered by December 23, 2026. The rule does not name China or any chip vendor; it gives only the general diversion-risk finding quoted above.

Semafor's Ashley Gold reported at 19:32 UTC that the company "at the heart of the Nvidia scandal" had been added to the list. BIS spokesman Thomas Wackman told Semafor: "The Commerce Department placed Megaspeed on the Entity List. This makes it unlawful to export any US-origin goods, software, or technology to Megaspeed," adding, "It is also now unlawful to export advanced SI chips to Megaspeed, even if the chips are produced outside the United States." Semafor reported that Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent had said earlier in November that Treasury, the Pentagon, the State Department and the US Trade Representative had backed listing Megaspeed "for months" but that "Commerce holds the pen," and that bipartisan lawmakers had pressed Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick to act.

Semafor tied the listing to the scandal around Nvidia's chief executive: Jensen Huang had resigned less than two weeks earlier amid scrutiny of his relationship with Alice Huang, a suspected Chinese spy who had been an executive at Megaspeed, and the board voted on November 23 to reinstate him after President Trump urged it to. According to Semafor, the Justice Department is investigating whether Megaspeed violated US export controls by shipping Nvidia chips to China; Megaspeed has denied wrongdoing. Bloomberg reported the coming listing the same afternoon, and on November 24 reported Singapore Prime Minister Lawrence Wong saying the city-state would not be used as a conduit to get around US chip curbs.

## Why It Matters

Entity List additions are routine BIS practice, but this one is the first US export restriction on Megaspeed, which Bloomberg had reported in December 2025 was Nvidia's biggest chip buyer in Southeast Asia and under US investigation over possible smuggling of chips to China. It moves the matter from investigation and interagency dispute, which Bessent's "Commerce holds the pen" remark had made public, to a binding export restriction that applies to US suppliers and, per BIS, to advanced chips made abroad.

The listing also shows the main enforcement problem in the chip export regime: high-end accelerators reaching China through cloud and data-center operators in third countries. The rule names entities in Singapore, Malaysia and Thailand, and Wong's statement the next day addressed Singapore's role as a possible transit point. The broader Nvidia governance and espionage questions, including the Justice Department inquiry and Huang's resignation and reinstatement, remain unresolved, and the rule itself says nothing about them.
